In brief

- AI until now **answered**. An agent **acts**: it signs in to systems, fills out a form, sends an email, changes data.
- That hasn't made it smarter. What changed everything is that its mistakes now have **consequences**.
- The question isn't whether we trust it, but **what it has permission to do** and **what it can't do without approval**.
- The rules are the same as for a new employee: narrow permissions, approval before irreversible steps, and a log of who did what.

</div>

Over the past few years, office artificial intelligence did one thing: it
returned text. You asked for a summary, you got one. You asked for a draft
email, you got one. What you did with the answer was up to you—you read it,
corrected it, sent it.

Now a new word is making the rounds in the industry: **agent**. The name may
sound mysterious, but what it means is simple. An agent doesn't make a
suggestion; it does the task. It doesn't write the email—it sends it. It
doesn't tell you what to enter into the system—it enters it.

## What's the difference between “answers” and “does it”?

At first it sounds like a matter of convenience: one less click. In practice,
though, an important safety element disappears from the process—**the person
who used to stand between the answer and the consequence**.

When AI only made suggestions, every one of its mistakes stopped at a human
glance. Was the amount wrong? You caught it before sending. Was the deadline
off? You fixed it. This filter wasn't perfect, but it was there.

With an agent, this step drops out—unless we deliberately put it back.

## Why now?

It didn't happen overnight. Two things came together.

One is that language models can now **break a task down into steps** reliably
enough: what to look at first, what comes next, when it's done. The other is
that, one after another, software products have gained connection points
through which a program can do the same things a person does by clicking in the
interface.

That is what suddenly made agents usable—and that is what made the question of
permissions urgent.

> **The turning point isn't that AI got smarter. It's that it no longer suggests—it presses the button.**

## The cost of a mistake has changed

A language model is sometimes confidently wrong. That was never good, but it
was manageable: a person read the text and corrected it. With an agent,
however, the mistake doesn't stay text—it becomes a **sent email**, **changed
data** or **an order**.

It's important to see that this isn't about AI “running amok.” The everyday
risks are far more mundane: it misunderstands the task, picks the wrong record,
does the same thing twice, or goes ahead with something you only asked it to
prepare.

## Not a question of trust, but of permissions

The most common mistake is that companies frame this as a question of trust:
“Can we trust it?” That's a dead end, because there's no good answer. The
useful question goes like this: **what can it do, and what does it need
permission for?**

This is exactly the same logic that works with a new employee. They don't get
narrower permissions because we don't trust them, but because that way their
mistakes do less damage, too.

| What you can safely hand over | What needs human approval |
|---|---|
| Collecting, extracting and comparing data | Sending an email to a client or partner |
| Preparing drafts, summaries, meeting minutes | Financial transactions, orders, contractual commitments |
| Organizing internal tasks, reminders | Changing or deleting data in a production system |
| Preparing recurring administrative work | Granting permissions, creating accounts |
| Suggesting what the next step should be | Anything visible outside the company |

The point of the right-hand column isn't that the agent shouldn't touch these
things. It's that **it can prepare them, but a person sends them.**

## Five rules before you switch it on

<ol class="lepesek">
<li><strong>Give it its own identity.</strong> Don't let it work under an employee's account, because then everything in the log appears under that person's name. With a separate identity, you can trace what the machine did and what the person did.</li>
<li><strong>Start with read-only access.</strong> For the first few weeks, it's enough for it to look, gather and suggest. What truly saves time often shows up at this level already.</li>
<li><strong>Draw the irreversible line.</strong> A sent email, a transfer, a deletion, a permission grant: these should always be preceded by human approval. This doesn't slow down the benefit; it only limits the damage.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a log, and have someone actually look at it.</strong> In the early period, it's worth skimming through what it did every week. Most misunderstandings come to light here—well before they turn into trouble.</li>
<li><strong>Say who it belongs to.</strong> Name an employee who is responsible for it: they shut it down if it behaves strangely, and they're the one people ask if something seems off. Systems without an owner cause the most trouble.</li>
</ol>

## And what's worth doing on the systems side

<details>
<summary>Technical details</summary>

- **A separate service account** for the agent, with the narrowest permissions needed—not a personal account, not administrator rights
- **Permission expiration and regular review**: what was needed for an experiment shouldn't still be active three months later
- **An approval step** before outbound and irreversible actions, technically enforced, not just requested in a policy
- **Full logging**: what it accessed, what it changed, when—in a searchable form
- **A separate test environment** for the first phase of the rollout, with no production data
- **Clarifying data handling**: what data can go to the service, where it is stored, for how long—this also goes into the internal policy
- **A way to stop it**: there should be a switch that stops it immediately, and someone should know where it is

</details>

## What to expect, realistically

Today, agents deliver tangible results in repetitive, well-defined
administration: they gather data, prepare and organize. What doesn't work:
taking over responsibility. Numbers, deadlines and commitments still have to be
approved by a person.

So the success of the rollout doesn't depend on which product you choose. It
depends on whether your permissions are in order, and whether you have decided
in advance where the line is.

> **An agent is exactly as dangerous as the permissions you gave it. That's not about the agent—it's about you.**
