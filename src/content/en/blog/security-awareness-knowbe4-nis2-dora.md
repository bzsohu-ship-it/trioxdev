Annual security training typically looks like this: there's a presentation in
January, everyone signs an attendance sheet, and the binder goes back on the
shelf. When an email that's indistinguishable from the real thing arrives in
March from the “supplier” with a new bank account number, nothing from that
presentation helps anymore.

This isn't the employees' fault. A one-off talk doesn't build a reflex—not to
mention that attacks have changed a lot in the meantime. We
[wrote about this separately](/en/blog/ai-voice-cloning-fraud/) earlier: bad
spelling as a warning sign is gone.

## “People are the weakest link”—a cliché, but measurable

This sentence has been written so many times that it has lost its weight. Yet
it has a practical consequence that is rarely discussed: **if people are one of
the carriers of risk, then that risk can also be measured.**

Not by asking who paid attention during the training, but by looking at what
people do in a realistic but harmless test situation. That's the difference
between “we held training” and “we know where we stand.”

> **If it isn't measured, we don't know whether it improved. If it isn't documented, the auditor assumes it never happened.**

## What the law requires—and what it doesn't

It's worth being precise here, because there are a lot of misconceptions about
this.

In Hungary, the NIS2 Directive was transposed by **Act LXIX of 2024** (the
Cybersecurity Act), which entered into force on January 1, 2025; for private
businesses, the Hungarian supervisory authority is the **SZTFH**. In addition,
the financial sector is subject to the **DORA** regulation—(EU) 2022/2554—which
has applied since January 17, 2025.

The two regulations approach training differently:

| | Who it applies to | What it says about training |
|---|---|---|
| **Cybersecurity Act (NIS2)** | The organizations in scope—typically in critical and important sectors | Cyber hygiene and training are among the required risk management measures. The implementing decree requires specific training, with a set number of hours, for **senior executives and information security officers**. |
| **DORA** | Financial entities | Article 13 explicitly requires an ICT security awareness program for **all employees and senior management**, with depth tailored to the role. |

The training hours for executives and security officers are set out in
**Decree 17/2025. (VII. 24.) EM**; it gives those appointed before the end of
2025 a transitional deadline. Exactly what applies to a given company depends
on its sector and size—**this question is worth clarifying with an expert; this
article is not legal advice.**

Two things are worth highlighting, though, because they hold for everyone in
scope:

- **Neither regulation prescribes specific software.** You can comply without
  an e-learning system.
- **Both, however, require that you can prove it.** In an inspection, what
  counts isn't intent—it's the documentation.

## What a platform adds

This is where the tool category comes in whose best-known representative is
**KnowBe4**. It isn't magic and it isn't mandatory—it simply does the three

The first is **regularity**: short, recurring training units instead of a

## Simulated phishing isn't a trap

This is the most common objection, and it's a fair one: nobody wants employees
to feel they're being set up. Experience shows that this depends on **how it's
introduced**.

What's worth saying up front: the goal isn't to shame employees, but for
mistakes to surface **in practice**, not in a real attack. Someone who clicks
should get immediate, matter-of-fact feedback—not a lecture. And results broken
down by name shouldn't end up on the bulletin board.

And the most important point: **reporting should count as success, not a
flawless record.** If an employee is afraid of the consequences, they won't
speak up in a real attack either—and those are exactly the few hours lost
during which the damage could still have been contained.

> **A simulation is good if, at the end, more people dare to speak up. Not if fewer people click.**

## How to get started

<ol class="lepesek">
<li><strong>Find out what applies to you.</strong> Sector, size, client base—these show whether the reason is the Cybersecurity Act, DORA, a client's vendor review or simply common sense. The answer determines how far you need to go.</li>
<li><strong>Measure a baseline.</strong> A first test covering everyone shows where the company stands today. Without it, there will be nothing to compare against later—and improvement is the best argument for continuing.</li>
<li><strong>Announce the rules in advance.</strong> That there will be tests, that their purpose is practice, and that reporting never leads to repercussions. This has to come from leadership, not from IT.</li>
<li><strong>Start with leadership.</strong> Not only because the stricter training requirements apply to them, but because they are also typically the target of impersonation fraud.</li>
<li><strong>Make it regular, and keep the reports.</strong> An audit or an insurance claim settlement asks for evidence after the fact. A report is only worth something if it still exists when nobody remembers the details anymore.</li>
</ol>

## What to think through on the systems side

<details>
<summary>Technical and rollout details</summary>

- **Hungarian-language content**: training only works if employees get it in their own language—the larger platforms, including KnowBe4, support this
- **Breakdown by role**: finance and leadership need different scenarios than production
- **Letting simulated emails through** the email system's filters, so that the test actually arrives—this takes planning
- **Clarifying data protection**: what data is generated about employees, who sees it, how long we keep it—this also goes into the internal policy and the employee privacy notice
- **A simple reporting path**: a button in the email client that reports a suspicious email in one click
- **Keeping reports** for as long as the audit requires, in a searchable form
- **Integration with existing protection**: training complements the technical layer, it doesn't replace it—without MFA, email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) and anti-phishing protection, it isn't enough on its own

</details>

## What not to expect from it

To be honest: an awareness program doesn't solve everything. It won't prevent
zero-day vulnerabilities, it doesn't replace backups, and it doesn't replace
multi-factor authentication. Anyone who hopes the click rate will drop to zero
after training will be disappointed—that isn't a realistic goal anyway.

What it does give you: a significant share of attacks stop at the employee,
and for the rest, **the report comes in hours sooner**. With ransomware or
payment fraud, those few hours decide how large the damage is.
